\title[Discovering Hidden Relations Among Triangle Invariants]%
{Discovering Hidden Relations\\[3pt] Among Triangle Invariants}
\author{triangle-relations project}
\date{}

\subjclass[2020]{51M04, 68T07}
\keywords{triangle geometry, Euler's relation, functional dependence, autoencoders, dimension counting}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We describe a numerical method for discovering unknown functional relations
among scalar quantities derived from a triangle, in the spirit of Euler's
relation between the circumradius, the inradius, and the incenter-to-
circumcenter distance. A triangle has three degrees of freedom, so a
relation among four or more derived scalars is guaranteed by dimension
counting alone and is uninformative; a relation among exactly three is a
genuine, generically unexpected coincidence. We detect such coincidences by
training a bottleneck autoencoder on sampled triangle data and comparing its
reconstruction error against a column-permuted null, and make the detector's
behavior mathematically precise. Applied to $(R,r,d)$, the method flags
exactly this triple as dependent, with no prior knowledge of Euler's
relation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Dimension counting}
\label{sec:setup}

A triangle, up to translation and rotation, has three degrees of freedom
(e.g.\ its three side lengths $a,b,c$); write $\M$ for this moduli space,
$\dim\M=3$. Every derived scalar --- area, the circumradius $R$ (the radius
of the circle through all three vertices), the inradius $r$ (the radius of
the circle inscribed in the triangle), distances between derived points,
\dots --- is a smooth function $f:\M\to\mathbb R$, and $k$ of them give a map
$F=(f_1,\dots,f_k):\M\to\mathbb R^k$. Call a property of $F$ \emph{generic}
if it holds outside a closed, measure-zero exceptional set of choices of the
$f_i$; for instance, the Jacobian $DF(p)$ (the $k\times3$ matrix of partial
derivatives $(\partial f_i/\partial x_j)$ in local coordinates on $\M$ at a
point $p$) generically has the largest rank its shape allows, $\min(k,3)$,
since failing to do so is a closed, codimension-$\ge1$ condition on the
$f_i$.

If $k>3$, take a point $p$ where $DF(p)$ has the generic rank $3$, i.e.\
$F$ is an immersion at $p$: by the constant rank theorem, in suitable local
coordinates near $p$ and $F(p)$, $F$ looks like the standard inclusion
$\mathbb R^3\hookrightarrow\mathbb R^k$. Consequently the image of a small
neighborhood of $p$ is a smooth $3$-dimensional submanifold, cut out there
by $k-3$ independent equations $G_i(f_1,\dots,f_k)=0$ in the $k-3$
coordinate directions transverse to it. These $k-3$ local relations arise
from dimension counting alone, \emph{for free}, regardless of which $f_i$
were chosen; a relation among four or more triangle invariants therefore
says nothing specific about triangle geometry.

If $k=3$, recall $F$ is a \emph{local diffeomorphism} at $p$ (a smooth
bijection near $p$ with smooth inverse) exactly when $DF(p)$ is invertible,
by the inverse function theorem. Generically $DF$ has full rank $3$, and
near such a point $(f_1,f_2,f_3)$ sweeps out an open subset of $\mathbb
R^3$ with nonempty interior --- a genuine $3$-dimensional volume, not a
lower-dimensional surface. An \emph{exact} relation $G(f_1,f_2,f_3)=0$
holding identically is equivalent to $DF$ having rank $\le2$ everywhere,
collapsing the image onto a $2$-dimensional surface: not generic, a genuine
algebraic coincidence. Euler's relation
\begin{equation}
  d^2=R^2-2Rr
\end{equation}
(where $d$ is the incenter-to-circumcenter distance) is exactly such a
statement about $(R,r,d)$. This is the sense in which a relation among
exactly three invariants --- not four --- is structurally new.

Is the solution set of Euler's relation actually a submanifold, or does it
have edges or cusps? Generically the former, with one exception.

\begin{proposition}[Smoothness of the Euler surface]\label{prop:eulersmooth}
Let $\Sigma=\{(R,r,d)\in\mathbb R^3 : d^2=R^2-2Rr\}$. Then $\Sigma$ is a
smooth (real-analytic) embedded $2$-dimensional submanifold of $\mathbb R^3$
away from the single point $0=(0,0,0)$, which is a genuine conical
singularity: writing $u=R-r$, Euler's relation becomes $u^2=r^2+d^2$, the
equation of a standard double cone with vertex at $u=r=d=0$. No actual
(nondegenerate) triangle reaches this vertex, since every triangle has
$R>0$ strictly, so the physically realized part of $\Sigma$ is smooth
throughout. It does have one edge --- not a cusp --- along the equilateral
locus $r=R/2$, $d=0$, where Euler's inequality $R\ge2r$ becomes an equality.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The gradient of $G(R,r,d)=R^2-2Rr-d^2$ is $\nabla G=(2R-2r,-2R,-2d)$, which
vanishes only when $R=r$, $R=0$, $d=0$ simultaneously, i.e.\ only at the
origin (which does lie on $\Sigma$, since $G(0,0,0)=0$). By the implicit
function theorem, $\Sigma$ is a smooth submanifold at every point where
$\nabla G\ne0$, hence everywhere except possibly the origin. Completing the
square, $G=(R-r)^2-r^2-d^2$, so with $u=R-r$, $\Sigma=\{u^2=r^2+d^2\}$: the
standard double cone. A punctured neighborhood of its vertex $u=r=d=0$ on
$\Sigma$ has two connected components (one per nappe, since $u=0$ forces
$r=d=0$ too), unlike a punctured disk in $\mathbb R^2$; so the vertex is not
even a topological manifold point, confirming it is a genuine singularity.

For an actual triangle, $R>0$ always (a nondegenerate triangle has positive
circumradius), so the physically realized points of $\Sigma$ never coincide
with the singular vertex. Writing $d=\sqrt{R(R-2r)}$ (the nonnegative root,
since $d$ is a distance) over the physical domain $\{R>0,\,0<r\le R/2\}$ ---
using Euler's inequality $R\ge2r$, which always holds for a genuine triangle
--- exhibits the physical surface as a smooth graph, with an edge exactly at
$r=R/2$ ($d=0$, the equilateral case). Viewed on the full cone $\Sigma$,
this locus is an ordinary smooth curve: $\nabla G\ne0$ there too, e.g.\ at
$(R,R/2,0)$ with $R>0$, $\nabla G=(R,-2R,0)\ne0$. It becomes an edge of the
\emph{physical} surface only because the mirror branch $d<0$ --- equally
part of $\Sigma$, but physically meaningless --- is discarded.
\end{proof}

\section{The autoencoder detector}
\label{sec:autoencoder}

Given a candidate triple $(f_1,f_2,f_3)$, sample many random triangles and
form the point cloud $X=\{(f_1,f_2,f_3)(T)\}\subset\mathbb R^3$. An
autoencoder --- an encoder $e:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ and decoder
$d:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3$ trained to minimize
$\mathbb E\|d(e(x))-x\|^2$ --- searches for the best rank-$2$ approximation to
$X$: if $X$ lies on a $2$-surface, the encoder need only learn a local chart
and the decoder its inverse, driving error to $0$; if $X$ fills an open
$3$-region, no map factoring through $\mathbb R^2$ can avoid discarding a
whole dimension, so error stays bounded away from $0$.

\subsection{A precise statement}

Let $P$ be the law of $(f_1,f_2,f_3)(T)$ and $X=\operatorname{supp}(P)$. Fix
input and output dimensions $p,q\ge1$ (for us, always $p,q\in\{2,3\}$). By a
\emph{two-layer perceptron of width $h$} we mean a map
$N:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^q$ of the form
$N(x)=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x+b_1)+b_2$, with $W_1\in\mathbb R^{h\times p}$,
$b_1\in\mathbb R^h$, $W_2\in\mathbb R^{q\times h}$, $b_2\in\mathbb R^q$
trainable and $\sigma=\tanh$ applied componentwise; write
$\mathcal N_h(\mathbb R^p,\mathbb R^q)$ for the class of all such maps
$\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^q$. Recall $g$ is \emph{$K$-Lipschitz} if
$\|g(x)-g(y)\|\le K\|x-y\|$; since $\tanh$ is $1$-Lipschitz, every two-layer
perceptron is Lipschitz, with constant controlled by $\|W_1\|,\|W_2\|$.
Train an \emph{encoder} $e\in\mathcal N_h(\mathbb R^3,\mathbb R^2)$ and a
\emph{decoder} $d\in\mathcal N_h(\mathbb R^2,\mathbb R^3)$ to minimize
$L(e,d)=\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\|d(e(x))-x\|^2$.

\begin{proposition}[Sufficiency]\label{prop:suff}
Recall a subset of $\mathbb R^n$ is \emph{compact} iff closed and bounded
(Heine--Borel). If $X$ is compact and admits continuous
$\varphi:X\to\mathbb R^2$, $\psi:\varphi(X)\to\mathbb R^3$ with
$\psi(\varphi(x))=x$ for all $x\in X$ --- in particular whenever an exact
relation confines $X$ to a $2$-dimensional set --- then for every
$\varepsilon>0$ there is a width $h$ and $(e,d)$ with $L(e,d)<\varepsilon$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991): for any
continuous $\varphi:K\to\mathbb R^q$ on compact $K$ and any $\delta>0$, some
$N\in\mathcal N_h(\mathbb R^p,\mathbb R^q)$ satisfies
$\sup_{x\in K}\|N(x)-\varphi(x)\|<\delta$. Approximating $\varphi$ on $X$ and
$\psi$ on $\varphi(X)$ gives $e,d$ with $\sup_{x\in X}\|d(e(x))-x\|\to0$,
hence $L(e,d)\to0$.
\end{proof}

In plain terms: if $(f_1,f_2,f_3)$ really do satisfy an exact relation, a
large enough autoencoder reconstructs them essentially perfectly, however
the relation happens to curve through $\mathbb R^3$ --- the encoder just
learns to read off a valid pair of local coordinates on that curved surface,
and the decoder learns to invert them. The next proposition is the converse:
without such a relation, no autoencoder of bounded complexity can do better
than some fixed, positive error, no matter how it is trained. ``Bounded
complexity'' here means a bound on the Lipschitz constant $K$ of $e$ and
$d$ (roughly, how fast their outputs can change relative to their inputs);
we explain right after the proof why this qualifier is unavoidable.

\begin{proposition}[Necessity, at bounded capacity]\label{prop:nec}
Fix $K>0$. Recall $P$ is \emph{absolutely continuous} (w.r.t.\ Lebesgue
measure) if it assigns zero probability to every Lebesgue-null set, i.e.\
has a density. If $X$ contains an open ball $B$ on which $P$ is absolutely
continuous with $P(B)>0$, there is $c=c(P|_B,K)>0$ with $L(e,d)\ge c$ for
every pair of $K$-Lipschitz $e,d$ --- in particular for every
$(e,d)\in\mathcal N_h(\mathbb R^3,\mathbb R^2)\times\mathcal
N_h(\mathbb R^2,\mathbb R^3)$ whose realized weights keep the Lipschitz
constant below $K$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The Hausdorff dimension of $S\subset\mathbb R^n$ generalizes ordinary
dimension to non-smooth sets (covering $S$ by $\varepsilon$-balls takes
$\sim\varepsilon^{-\dim S}$ of them); Lipschitz maps do not increase it. So
$d(\mathbb R^2)$ has Hausdorff dimension $\le2$, hence is Lebesgue-null in
$\mathbb R^3$, hence $P$-null on $B$; thus $\operatorname{dist}(x,d(\mathbb
R^2))>0$ for $P|_B$-a.e.\ $x$, and a compactness argument over $K$-Lipschitz
maps gives $\mathbb E_{x\sim P|_B}\operatorname{dist}(x,d(\mathbb
R^2))^2\ge c>0$. Since $d(e(\mathbb R^3))\subseteq d(\mathbb R^2)$, the same
bound applies to $L(e,d)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
By Brouwer's invariance-of-domain theorem, a continuous injection between
open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$ is an open map, so no continuous injection of
$\mathbb R^2$ into $\mathbb R^3$ has open image: exact space-filling is
topologically impossible regardless of capacity. What is not ruled out is
\emph{approximate} space-filling by increasingly large-Lipschitz-constant
maps as $K\to\infty$, which is why Proposition~\ref{prop:nec} fixes $K$; in
practice $\ell_2$ weight regularization and early stopping keep the realized
Lipschitz constant controlled.
\end{remark}

Propositions~\ref{prop:suff} and \ref{prop:nec} say that, at any fixed,
regularized capacity, reconstruction error going to $0$ versus staying
bounded away from it is decided entirely by whether $(f_1,f_2,f_3)$ satisfy
an exact relation --- the theoretical content behind the detection rule used
throughout. Figure~\ref{fig:surface-vs-volume} shows this dichotomy on the
actual triple $(R,r,d)$.

\section{Calibration via a permutation null}
\label{sec:null}

Propositions~\ref{prop:suff} and \ref{prop:nec} describe an idealized limit
($h\to\infty$, exact expectations); in practice we train one finite network
on one finite sample, and its reconstruction loss $L_{\mathrm{real}}$ is
never exactly $0$ or exactly the theoretical floor $c$ --- it is a single
noisy number. An absolute threshold on $L_{\mathrm{real}}$ (``flag it as
dependent if the loss is below $0.01$'', say) is therefore not meaningful:
different triples of scalars differ in units and scale, network training is
itself noisy, and, since all derived scalars are functions of the same
three underlying triangle parameters, even functionally \emph{independent}
triples can be mildly correlated and so reconstruct a bit better than one
might naively expect. What we need is not one global cutoff but a
baseline for ``no relation'' calibrated separately for each triple.

We obtain that baseline by destroying exactly the joint structure between
the three scalars while leaving everything else about the data unchanged.
Concretely, write the sample as a matrix $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times3}$, one row
per sampled triangle. Form a \emph{shuffled} copy $X'$ by independently and
uniformly permuting the $n$ entries within each of the three columns
(a different random permutation per column). Each column of $X'$ is some
reordering of the corresponding column of $X$, so it has exactly the same
values, and hence the same one-dimensional marginal distribution, as
before; but the pairing \emph{across} columns is now uniformly random, so
whatever functional relationship held between $f_1,f_2,f_3$ in the real
data has been destroyed. Training the same encoder/decoder architecture on
$X'$ therefore measures how well an autoencoder can compress three
quantities that merely share these marginal distributions but have no
functional relationship at all --- precisely the situation of
Proposition~\ref{prop:nec}.

We repeat the shuffle-and-train step $m$ times (an independent random
reshuffling of $X$ each time), obtaining $m$ null reconstruction losses
$L'_1,\dots,L'_m$, and record their sample mean and sample standard
deviation,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{null}}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m L'_i,
  \qquad
  \sigma_{\mathrm{null}}=\left(\frac1{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^m
    (L'_i-\mu_{\mathrm{null}})^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
These summarize the null distribution of reconstruction loss for this
particular triple's marginals: $\mu_{\mathrm{null}}$ is its typical value
under ``no relation'', and $\sigma_{\mathrm{null}}$ is how much that value
fluctuates from one reshuffling (and training run) to the next. We compare
the real loss to this null distribution via the standard $z$-score,
\begin{equation}
  z=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{null}}-L_{\mathrm{real}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{null}}} ,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the number of null standard deviations by which the real loss falls
below the null mean, or equivalently via the ratio
$L_{\mathrm{real}}/\mu_{\mathrm{null}}$. A large $z$ (ratio close to $0$)
means the real triple reconstructs far better than shuffled versions of
itself typically do: the fingerprint of Proposition~\ref{prop:suff} taking
over, and our evidence of an unexpected functional relation. A $z$ near $0$
(ratio close to $1$) means the real data compresses no better than its own
null, consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:nec} and no relation. Because
$\mu_{\mathrm{null}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{null}}$ are computed separately
for each candidate triple, from data with the same units, scale, and
marginal shape as the real sample, this comparison is self-calibrating and
needs no triple-independent threshold.

\section{The pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Enumerate candidate triples of derived scalars.
  \item Sample random triangles once per triple; train a $2$-bottleneck
    autoencoder; compare held-out error to a column-shuffled null (several
    repeats).
  \item Rank triples by real/null ratio (small $=$ strong candidate).
\end{enumerate}
The autoencoder is purely a screening tool: it flags \emph{whether} a triple
is suspiciously dependent, not what the relation is. The
\texttt{verify\_euler\_relation} module runs this on $(R,r,d)$, confirming
it flags Euler's relation with no prior knowledge of the formula.
